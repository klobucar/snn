Creature Feature - Osprey by Michelle Berns
    Found on every continent except Antarctica, these beautiful birds have a wingspan
of five to six feet and weigh between 2.5-4.5 pounds. Females are the larger of the
sexes and have a gorgeous necklace of darker plumage across their chest. Males have
a pure white chest. Both have dark brown feathers on their back and wings with a
white head. Adults have vibrant yellow eyes. Young have orange eyes and have white
tipped edges on their dark wings. In flight these birds look like an “M”, with a dark
wrist patch under their wings.
    Ospreys migrate to central and South America each fall. The male returns from
his overwintering grounds around April and tidies up the nest, which can weigh up to
a hundred pounds and be 3-6 feet across. The female will help finalize the nest
renovations and then lay two to four eggs over the course of several days. Both
parents take turns incubating the eggs for 36-42 days. After 55 days the young will
fledge. They follow the adults to the overwintering grounds but remain there for
another whole year before coming back to their hatch area. It is the male that exhibits
the highest nest fidelity, meaning that where he hatches, he will come back to. Choosing a mate and starting
the lifecycle over again happens around four to five years of age.
    Ospreys are sometimes called a fish hawk, sea hawk, or river hawk because of their specialized diet of
fish. Ospreys have many adaptations to help them hunt and hold their prey. Great vision and specialized oil
droplets in their retina help them to see fish underwater from 130 feet up in the air without the glare of the
sun! Similar to how you see with polarized lenses. They dive into the water feet first with nostrils that close
to keep the water out. They also have extra oily plumage to keep water from penetrating their feathers.
    Like other raptors, they have great talons to help hold their prey. Osprey have another adaptation for
grasping slippery fish, an outer toe that rotates for two in front and two in back (Zygodactyl) as well as
spicules (raised bumps for an improved grip). They carry fish headfirst to help with aerodynamics.
                                                           Until recently, Osprey had not nested in Iowa since
                                                      before European settlement. The Iowa DNR began a
                                                      reintroduction program in 1997 at Lake McBride in
                                                      Johnson County. The program continued through 2016 at
                                                      11 other places throughout Iowa.
                                                           There were 301 juvenile Osprey brought in from
                                                      Minnesota and Wisconsin at 42 days old and placed in
                                                      hack towers. The young Osprey were fed through PVC
                                                      tubes so they would not become imprinted on humans.
                                                      Fledging happened around 55 days and they eventually
                                                      learned to catch their own fish. In the meantime, fish were
                                                      still placed in the hack towers as the doors were open and
                                                      they were free to come and go.

     The first nesting’s of wild Osprey in Iowa took place in Spirit Lake and Iowa
City in 2003. Since then, Osprey have been very successful from this
reintroduction. Nests are monitored every year by the Iowa DNR with the help of
volunteers. During the 2025 nesting season, 70 nests were monitored; 54 were
active nests. Thirty of the nests fledged 58 Osprey!
     Since acquiring Deep Lakes Park in 2012, staff have seen many Ospreys flying
and fishing especially around Lake Chester. In an effort to help these incredible
birds of prey, we installed two nesting platforms in the park. It is our hope that we
will attract a couple returning males to take advantage of the platforms (see photo
left). One is situated near the trail overlooking Lake Ivy and the other is North of
the Cabins, near the bike trail between Lakes Chester and Patricia.
     This project could not have come together without partnerships! We are
incredibly thankful for our talented maintenance crew who built the 5-foot
diameter nesting platforms.
     We also want to thank Eastern Iowa Light and Power (REC) for donating
both of the 30-foot power poles the platforms were placed on.
     Another huge THANKS goes out to Muscatine Power and Water for allowing
their very talented and efficient crew to spend two mornings installing the poles
with the nesting platforms already attached.
     It is our hope that multiple generations of Osprey can use the towers and that visitors to Deep Lakes Park
will enjoy watching these magnificent raptors.



MOTUS Installation at Discovery Park - by Michelle Berns
    Our partnership with Muscatine Power and Water and Eastern
Iowa REC also allowed us to install a power pole in Discovery Park
that will be connected by an antenna to an international research
network called MOTUS. This program uses radio telemetry to track
                                       birds, bats, and large insects,
                                       including Monarch Butterflies!
                                          When a tagged creature flies
                                       by a motus station (which are
                                       located from Canada to South
                                       America) it pings the antennae
                                       and information is uploaded to the cloud.
                                          Scientists are using the data that is gathered to answer some great
                                       questions about migration, life cycles, animal physiology, and urban
                                       effects just to name a few. To date there have been 1,246 projects, over
                                       504 species, and 66,815 individuals tagged. Thirty-four (34) countries
                                       are included with this research and there are 2,472 MOTUS stations.
                                          We are excited to be able to use this data in our environmental
                                       education programs. MOTUS.org is where you can find more
                                       information about this program.

Park Spotlight: Discovery Park
   Discovery Park is a 100-acre county park located just inside the Muscatine city limits on Cedar Street, near
Highway 61. This multi-use park is a great place to visit during every season.
    Discovery Park is open daily, year-round from 6:00 am – 10:30 pm. Here is a list of the top 10 things to do
at Discovery Park!
1. Visit the Environmental Learning Center. FREE and open to the public! It includes two 1,200 gallon fish
   aquariums, over 100 mounted
   animals, a kid’s exploration
   area, hands-on displays and
   more!
2. View live animals including
   raptors, fish, snakes, turtles,
   and more!
3. Attend a Naturalist led program
   or hike. Events are listed on
   Facebook and the website.
4. Hold a snake!
5. View fall colors at the
   Muscatine Arboretum. This
   living museum has trees and
   shrubs of every color, shape,
   and size.
6. Take a hike or bicycle on 2+
   miles of hard-surfaced trails
   that wind past prairie,
   woodlands, and ponds.
7. Bait a hook, sink a line, and try
   your luck at catching a fish or
   two at Discovery Pond &
   Heron Hideaway.
8. Enjoy some creative play on
   the nature themed playground.
9. Enjoy a picnic in the park.
10. Step back in time and enjoy a
    historic tour of the Old Barn.

              A Turtle Success Story!                                           Muscatine, IA 52761
    Back in June, Nate Barnett, a Wildlife Biologist                               563-264-5922
from Port Louisa National Wildlife Refuge spotted a
Mama Blandings turtle digging a nest on the edge of a
gravel road. Not the best place in terms of safety. He
                                                                 Conservation@muscatinecountyiowa.gov
knew he needed to do something to help this nest of rare
and threatened turtles survive.
    Huge THANKS goes out to the Muscatine County
Highway Department for avoiding major work in the
section of road during the incubation period.
    In order to give the turtles the best chance of
survival, the two turtles who hatched and the remaining
viable eggs were relocated to the Blank Park Zoo for
their Head-Start program. Turtles experience the highest
mortality in their first year of life. In a year, they will be
released back to the same general area where they were
found.
    Blanding’s turtles live in wetlands, spending most of
their time in the water. They are omnivores, eating all
sorts of things including small fish, insects, and aquatic
plants. They can live to be 80 or 90 years old in the wild.
    Muscatine County has an adult Blanding’s turtle
named Mr. B who can be visited at the Environmental
Learning Center.                                                            Sharing a love for snakes!
